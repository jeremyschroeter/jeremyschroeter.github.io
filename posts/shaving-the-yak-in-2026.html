The term "shaving the yak" refers to a type of procrastination, a type of procrastination that we are all too familiar with. I usually describe it to people as "cleaning your room before you do your homework", or "running the laundry before you do your taxes". It's not that cleaning one's room or running the laundry are unimportant, but relative to the task they displace they are obviously less important. In choosing to do an otherwise undesirable task over the more laborious, yet more important one (not to say that some of us don't enjoy cleaning our room, or doing the laundry), we procrastinate. We avoid the things we need to do.

When we "shave the yak", we procrastinate, but unlike normal procrastination, we work on tasks we would usually procrastinate.

As a PhD student in 2026, AI coding agents have caused me to wrestle with this dilemma far too often. I am an experimental neuroscientist; beyond the things I do with my hands, there isn't much an AI coding agent can't do faster and better than I can. Whether it be querying data, making plots, building a complicated analysis pipeline... whatever really. The computer will do it faster, almost always better, and all I need to do is say in plain language exactly what I want.

Everytime time I write my own code, am I shaving the yak?

So much of learning is struggling through the crucial steps of not knowing how anything works and trying to figure it out. Being thrown in the deep end. Reading documentation, consulting forums, talking to experts and peers in real life, and of course writing code. AI eliminates all of this. In these ways it forces us to do two things: (1) if we want to really understand how our code works, we must force ourselves to write it ourselves; and (2) it alienates us from the community of experts that learned before these tools existed. We may be able to ameliorate (2) through babysitting agents and treating them like a peer we are interacting with, but in my experience, going back and forth with Claude or ChatGPT is rarely the same experience as sitting in person with a peer.

Using AI for coding, I worry about my peers' and my ability to understand the world we are building. I am merely a neuroscientist; I like to think the work I do is important, but compared to that of an engineer, or frankly anyone else whose job interfaces with real people, it's peanuts.

As I write this I am reminded of the NASA code guidebook. Not for any particular entry in it or any rule it contains, but as a reminder that people must understand how what they build works. I am deeply concerned we are creating a world where not only the layman doesn't understand how the technology that runs the world works, but that eventually no one will...
